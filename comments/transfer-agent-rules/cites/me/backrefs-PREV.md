He describes formulas determining the required amount and says Robinhood's obligation rose from the low hundreds of millions into billions. He argues the formulas did not adequately account for a situation like GameStop. He does not identify a specific rule in that passage.

At approximately 12:25–12:39, he says continuing to trade in violation of capital requirements could have created a solvency issue and forced a trading halt across all accounts. These are paraphrases of his interview claims, rather than findings that the claims are legally or factually established.

## SEC net capital and clearinghouse collateral

Broker-dealer net capital requirements and NSCC clearinghouse deposit requirements are distinct obligations. The immediate documented pressure on Robinhood in January 2021 was a clearinghouse collateral demand. Calling this simply a post-2008 brokerage capital requirement obscures that distinction.

The SEC adopted the uniform net capital rule in 1975, decades before the 2008 crisis. That history does not rule out later amendments or post-crisis changes to clearinghouse risk requirements; it does mean that the basic brokerage net capital framework did not originate in 2008. See the [SEC's 1975 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/files/1975.pdf).

In his February 18, 2021 congressional testimony, Tenev stated: “Robinhood Securities remained in compliance with SEC net capital rules at all relevant times.” That is Robinhood's own representation, and it distinguishes an actual net capital breach from a hypothetical future problem. See [Tenev's written testimony, footnote 19](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA00/20210218/111207/HHRG-117-BA00-Wstate-TenevV-20210218.pdf).

## What the 1975 net capital rule required

SEC Rule 15c3-1 required a cushion of liquid capital after liabilities and regulatory deductions. The principal historical calculations were:

| Method | Historical requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Basic method | Aggregate indebtedness could not exceed 15 times net capital: net capital had to be at least 6⅔% of aggregate indebtedness, subject to the applicable minimum dollar requirement. |
| Alternative method | Eligible firms electing this method generally had to maintain the greater of $100,000 or 4% of customer-related aggregate debit items calculated under the customer reserve formula. |

These are historical thresholds, not a statement of the requirements applicable to Robinhood in 2021 or today. See the [1975 adopting release, 40 FR 29795](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1975-07-16/pdf/FR-1975-07-16.pdf) and the [SEC's contemporary description of the alternative method](https://www.sec.gov/about/annual_report/1977.pdf).

Net capital is not simply accounting equity or a cash balance. The calculation deducts illiquid assets and applies prescribed discounts, called haircuts, to securities positions. This provides a cushion against liquidation losses. Aggregate indebtedness is also a defined regulatory measure with exclusions, so the basic method was not a blanket 15:1 ceiling on all balance-sheet borrowing.

For illustration, $15 million of counted aggregate indebtedness would require at least $1 million of net capital under the basic ratio, assuming no higher applicable requirement. See [FINRA's Rule 15c3-1 text and interpretations](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/SEA.Rule_.15c3-1.Interpretations.pdf).

## How GME's price could affect the calculations

GME's rising price would not automatically change the rule's percentages. It could nevertheless change a broker's calculated capital position or dollar requirement, depending on the firm's exposures and activity:

- The broker's own GME positions could generate gains or losses and changing dollar deductions for securities positions, including applicable concentration charges.
- Additional customer borrowing to purchase GME could increase customer debit balances and therefore the required capital under the alternative method. A higher share price alone does not necessarily increase an existing customer's loan balance.
- Customer holdings rising in value do not, by themselves, establish that the broker's net capital requirement increased or that a breach occurred.

These are possible mechanisms, not evidence that they caused Robinhood to breach the net capital rule. The applicable rule can remain unchanged while the financial inputs, and thus the calculated amounts, change.

## The initial collateral demand was reduced

The House Financial Services Committee staff investigation found an initial NSCC deposit requirement of approximately $3.7 billion, against about $696 million already deposited, leaving roughly $3 billion to post. At 9:11 a.m. Eastern on January 28, 2021, before the market opened, NSCC waived the excess capital premium and reduced the total requirement to approximately $1.4 billion. Robinhood then wired approximately $734 million to satisfy the remaining deficit. The final total requirement and the additional cash needed were therefore different figures.

The report explains that failure to meet the unwaived demand could have constituted a serious NSCC rule violation and jeopardized Robinhood's clearing access. The waiver did not eliminate all collateral obligations. Nor does the waiver alone establish that unrestricted buying afterward would have been financially sustainable. See the [House investigation, especially pp. 54 and 63–64](https://democrats-financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/6.22_hfsc_gs.report_hmsmeetbp.irm.nlrf.pdf).

The supported distinction is that Robinhood faced real clearinghouse collateral pressure while, according to Tenev's testimony, remaining compliant with SEC net capital rules. The interview's broader framing does not by itself establish that post-2008 regulations legally required the particular purchase restrictions Robinhood chose.
