> CCS is now working on plans to become a co-transfer agent, i.e., it would assume some of the actual transfer work such as certificate issuance but would permit the banks to maintain the records and the formal title of transfer agent. “I’d rather not elaborate on this,” says William Dentzer when asked about the arrangement. “It’s a sensitive area and I don’t want to screw up our relations with the banks. But look, I’m not trying to build an empire. I’m just trying to get a job accomplished.” CCS recognizes that even certificate issuance will decline as certificate movement is reduced, but they know that assumption of that role could be an important precedent—which is just the reason it will almost certainly be resisted by the banks. “Once an organization like that gets a foot in the door,” says a bank executive, “there’s no telling what might happen.”

> Asked about the possibility of CCS becoming a co-transfer agent, Edward Brown of Phillips Petroleum, who is vice chairman of the 100-member Corporate Transfer Agents Association, replies, “No way. We'd lose control. Who would take the responsibility if they goof up? There is no way I'd let them have any of our stock.” What about CCS as the sole transfer agent? “Hah, hah,” Brown laughs. “That is ridiculous. If they were the transfer agent for every issue, it would be the biggest pile of spaghetti you've ever seen. It would be a hairy ape. It would be like World War II.” It was precisely to extricate themselves from the Wall Street mess and deal with stockholders directly instead of through layers of bureaucracy that many corporations took back the transfer powers. It does not appear they will soon give that power up. Their resistance could doom CCS aspirations, for the ranks of corporate transfer agents can be said to carry a fair amount of influence. Among them are: AT&T, General Motors, Ford, U.S. Steel, General Electric, RCA, IBM, du Pont and ITT.

> Even to disinterested experts, the idea of CCS—an organization which has just recently recovered from a near total bookkeeping breakdown and which was created by an industry which twice within the past fifteen years has been wracked by operational chaos—becoming a giant behemoth handling shareholder records, or even certificate issuance, for thousands of the nation's major corporations gives a certain pause. “It is just not good business judgment for everybody on Wall Street to be dependent on a single entity,” says Eli Weinberg, a securities industry expert at Coopers & Lybrand (formerly Lybrand, Ross Bros. & Montgomery) and noted CCS critic.
> 
> Even if CCS could handle the chore, a serious question concerns whether it could adapt to what will certainly be the rapidly changing structure of the evolving central marketplace and whether, as presumably an officially sanctioned monopoly, it could avoid being beset with the sort of inefficiencies which lack of competition tends to breed. CCS argues that ownership by "users" would act as a cost control. But one operations expert responds that lacking any competitive facility to serve as a basis for comparison, CCS users would simply pass on whatever fees CCS charges to investors. "The real users," he says, "are the 32 million individual shareholders. They're the ones who will be screwed.
