The report identifies three important differences under French insolvency law as analyzed in 2009: whether the collateral is cash or securities, whether ownership was transferred or merely pledged, and where the securities account is located.

## 1. Cash versus securities

| Collateral arrangement                         | Treatment if French clearing member fails                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cash, even if pledged                          | Generally treated as commingled. Customer becomes an unsecured creditor, sharing losses with the member's other unsecured creditors.   |
| Securities transferred by title                | Customer generally becomes an unsecured creditor because ownership was transferred to the clearing member.                             |
| Securities pledged and held in customer's name | Customer retains proprietary rights. A securities shortfall is shared proportionally among customers holding that particular security. |

These are the report's default analyses where special financial-collateral protections do not apply.&#x20;

## 2. What difference does that make?

Suppose customers collectively own 100,000 shares of a particular stock, but the clearing member's custodian holds only 80,000.

Under the French securities segregation arrangement:

- Every qualifying customer receives 80% of their shares.
- The missing 20% becomes an unsecured claim against the failed clearing member.
- The customers do not automatically absorb losses from unrelated securities or the member's general debts through that securities allocation.

Under the unsecured-creditor arrangement: the customer has no comparable right to recover a specified portion of the securities pool and instead participates in the general insolvency distribution.

The report specifically says the securities shortfall is shared even among customers who did not authorize rehypothecation.&#x20;

## 3. France versus England

The report explains that a French bank might have securities booked at its English branch.

- Account in France: French law generally determines customer rights.
- Qualifying securities account in England: French insolvency courts would look to English law to determine those rights, even though the French bank itself is in French insolvency proceedings.
- Cash at an English branch: The report still generally treats customers as unsecured creditors under French insolvency law.

The English location does not automatically guarantee better protection. It changes which law governs the relevant securities rights.&#x20;
