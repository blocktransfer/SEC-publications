                                                                    Yes. There **are 1962-era drafting materials**, and they make the history much clearer. The important qualification is that **banks were not originally excluded from the depository mechanism itself**. They were excluded from **owning/control of the entity that qualified as a UCC “clearing corporation.”**

                                                                    The original 1962 § 8-102(3) said:

                                                                    > a “clearing corporation” is a corporation all of the capital stock of which is held by or for a national securities exchange or association registered under [federal law].

                                                                    At the same time, § 8-102(4) separately defined a **“custodian bank”** as a supervised bank or trust company acting as custodian for the clearing corporation. So the architecture was roughly:

                                                                    **exchange/NASD → owns clearing corporation → clearing corporation can use regulated banks as custodians.**

### The best 1962 evidence

Carlos Israels's **1962 article written while these changes were being made** is particularly useful. He discusses the New York provisions that became §§ 8-102(3), 8-102(4), and 8-320. He says the New York definition initially required the clearing corporation to be wholly owned by a **national securities exchange**, and reports that the Permanent Editorial Board decided to broaden it only to include ownership by a federally registered **association of securities dealers**, specifically so that the system could also serve the **over-the-counter market**. ([Cornell Law Scholarship][1])

                                                                    That is significant. In 1962, when the PEB actively reconsidered the ownership restriction, the contemplated expansion was:

                                                                    **NYSE-type exchange → exchange OR NASD-type dealer association**

                                                                    —not banks, trust companies, insurers, investment companies, etc.

                                                                    [Carlos L. Israels, Investment Securities in New York: Statutory Text and Commercial Practice, 48 Cornell L Rev 108 (1962)](https://scholarship.law.cornell.edu/clr/vol48/iss1/4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

                                                                    There's also a near-contemporaneous explanation of the 1962 amendments in the *Boston College Industrial & Commercial Law Review*. Its “Reason for and Probable Effect of the Change” says § 8-320 was intended to approve the newly developing method of transfers and pledges **“by brokers and banks within a central depository system.”** ([Lira][2])

                                                                    That is strong evidence against reading § 8-102(3) as an intention to keep banks *out of book-entry securities holding*. Banks were contemplated users/custodians. The restriction concerned **who could own/control the central depository whose entries were accorded the extraordinary legal effect of delivery.**

                                                                    ### And we actually have an explicit explanation of *why*

                                                                    The clearest historical statement I found comes from BASIC—the Banking and Securities Industry Committee—which later documented the creation of the system. It describes the original § 8-102(3) limitation as a **safeguard**, then says:

                                                                    > “This ownership restriction was logical when the UCC amendment was drafted, since book-entry transfers were being contemplated only between broker/dealers.”

                                                                    That is almost exactly the answer to your question. 

                                                                    BASIC then explains what changed after the 1968–69 paperwork crisis: a broker-only depository wasn't enough. Banks held huge amounts of securities and processed institutional deliveries, so the depository system needed to encompass the whole financial community. 

                                                                    Even more interestingly, BASIC says banks were initially uneasy about surrendering physical custody of securities for which they had fiduciary/custodial responsibility. Banks came to regard two conditions as important:

                                                                    1. the depository should be organized under banking law and supervised by banking authorities; and
                                                                    2. **banks should participate in the ownership and control of the depository they used.**

                                                                    BASIC expressly says the second point **required amendment of § 8-102(3)**. 

                                                                    So the later broadening wasn't really “we mistakenly discriminated against banks in 1962.” It was a change in the **conception of the depository**:

                                                                    **1962:** central book-entry mechanism for broker/dealer settlement
                                                                    → **1968–70:** comprehensive securities depository serving brokers + banks + institutional markets
                                                                    → therefore **1971–72:** ownership definition had to expand.

                                                                    ### There are actual PEB archival drafting files too

                                                                    The **Permanent Editorial Board's Report No. 1 (1962)** is the formal source covering the amendments leading to the 1962 Official Text. Berkeley has the cataloged 141-page report online. ([Lawcat][3])

                                                                    [1962 PEB Report No. 1 — Berkeley Law catalog](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/370392?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

                                                                    And the Soia Mentschikoff papers at Chicago contain what may be even better material if you want the internal rationale:

                                                                    * **Box 57, Folder 3:** PEB correspondence and memoranda, 1959–64
                                                                    * **Box 57, Folder 4:** first-meeting minutes, 1961–62
                                                                    * **Box 57, Folder 5:** **“Report from Subcommittee on Articles 3, 4, 5, 8, 1962”**
                                                                    * **Box 57, Folder 8:** Report No. 1, 1962
                                                                    * **Box 43, Folder 9:** NCCUSL Commercial Code Committee reports/correspondence, 1961–65. ([University of Chicago Library][4])

                                                                    [Soia Mentschikoff Papers finding aid — University of Chicago](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/e/scrc/findingaids/view.php?eadid=ICU.SPCL.MENTSCHIKOFF&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

                                                                    So **yes, the drafting trail exists**. What I have *not* yet found in searchable 1962 material is a PEB sentence literally saying “banks may not own a clearing corporation because X.” But the evidence converges very strongly: **the first central-depository provision was modeled around exchange/dealer clearing, not around a universal financial-industry depository; banks were contemplated as custodians/users, while exchange or NASD control served as the limiting safeguard.**

                                                                    And the later people who actually built the comprehensive depository system expressly described **that limited broker/dealer conception as the reason for the original ownership restriction**.
