# Rule 17Ad-17: Reconsider the $25 de minimis thresholds

## Current rule

Rule 17Ad-17 contains **two distinct $25 exemptions**:

1. **Lost securityholder searches — 17 CFR 240.17Ad-17(a)(3)(ii).** A transfer agent, broker, or dealer need not perform the two otherwise-required database searches where the *aggregate value* of the lost securityholder's account—securities plus dividend, interest, and other payments due—is **less than $25**. An account worth exactly $25 is not exempt. This is an exception to the specified searches, not a declaration that a small account is abandoned or that its owner has no rights.
2. **Unresponsive payee notices — 17 CFR 240.17Ad-17(c)(4).** A paying agent need not send the notice required by paragraph (c)(1) when the **unnegotiated check** is worth less than $25. This is a separate test of a payment, not of the entire account.

The distinction matters: raising, eliminating, or retaining one threshold need not entail the same treatment of the other. Nor do these federal exceptions themselves determine whether property is escheatable under state law.

**Source:** [Rule 17Ad-17 (current codified text)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.17Ad-17).

## The original $25 rationale: a 1997 cost-benefit judgment

In adopting Rule 17Ad-17, the Commission explained that **most commenters favored a $100-per-account de minimis threshold**, but selected **$25** because database searches were understood to be inexpensive. The chosen exception was meant to avoid searches that would not be cost-effective while preserving investor protection. *See* [62 FR 52231, middle column](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1997-10-07/pdf/97-26519.pdf#page=3).

The original cost-benefit analysis assumed a **60% success rate**: locating an account containing $25 would produce an expected $15 in recovered assets, above the estimates of search cost. The Commission estimated **$3.38 per lost account in the first year** and **$1.79 in subsequent years**. These are historical assumptions, not evidence of 2026 pricing or recovery performance. *See* [62 FR 52235](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1997-10-07/pdf/97-26519.pdf#page=7).

This history suggests the $25 figure was an *administrative cost-versus-recovery judgment*, not a legislative statement that accounts below $25 are immaterial. A modern review should test the original assumptions against actual search prices, success rates, administrative expenses, and the value of restored investor contact beyond immediate account value.

## Significance for the 2026 proposal

The September 4, 2026 proposed transfer-agent rules would add an **inactive securityholder** notification requirement. The proposed amendments would renumber existing paragraph (a)(3) as **(a)(4)** and make its existing exceptions apply not only to lost-securityholder database searches but also to the new inactive-holder notices. The proposal does **not** expressly replace the $25 amount. *See* [91 FR 57057–57058, proposed Rule 17Ad-17(a)(3)–(4)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/04/2026-18190/transfer-agent-rules).

This means the consequences of the threshold may broaden even though its nominal figure is unchanged. Evaluate the economic and investor-protection effects of excluding low-value accounts from preventive inactivity outreach, especially where securities can appreciate or where a low nominal balance may conceal an enduring ownership interest.

## Questions and possible comment positions

- **Reassessment:** Does the original $25 exemption still reflect modern costs of bulk database searches and electronic outreach, and what evidence supports keeping that figure rather than raising, lowering, or eliminating it?
- **Account value versus future rights:** Does the value at the time of an attempted search adequately capture dividends, future appreciation, voting rights, and the risk of involuntary liquidation through escheatment? Distinguish these potential harms from the rule's narrow minimum search obligation.
- **Notification versus database searching:** Should an exemption justified by paid database-search costs also excuse inexpensive electronic notices to inactive holders? Consider separate thresholds or different treatment for the two obligations.
- **Valuation mechanics:** How should agents treat illiquid, thinly traded, restricted, nonpublic, and tokenized securities when testing the aggregate-value limit? Does a zero or unavailable quoted price create an inappropriate path to exclusion?
- **Evidence and design:** Request data on lost/inactive accounts below $25, search and notification costs, successful reconnections, the number and value of positions ultimately escheated, and whether automated contact methods could protect small holders without imposing disproportionate costs.
- **Avoid overclaiming:** The exemption says agents *need not* perform the listed steps; it does not prohibit voluntary searches or notices, extinguish beneficial ownership, or preempt applicable state unclaimed-property requirements.

A defensible comment could ask the Commission **to explain and substantiate its decision to carry the 1997 dollar threshold into the expanded 2026 rule**, and to consider distinguishing database-search costs from the costs of electronic notifications. It should not assert that every low-value account must receive a paid search without first analyzing the costs and expected results.
