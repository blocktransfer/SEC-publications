# 1933 basis of Securities Act § 4(a)(1): distribution versus trading

The current Securities Act § 4(a)(1), 15 USC 77d(a)(1), exempts "transactions by any person other than an issuer, underwriter, or dealer." The important historical point is that this classification did not originate with the SEC. It was congressional language from the original 1933 legislation, before the SEC existed, and the House report described it as the line between securities _distribution_ and ordinary _trading_. The FTC was the original federal administrator of that statutory scheme.

## Drafting chain in 1933

- **March 29, 1933 — H.R. 4314.** Rep. Sam Rayburn introduced H.R. 4314, the initial House Federal Securities Act bill. The precursor used an "isolated transactions" approach rather than the later H.R. 5480 formulation. The House Committee held hearings on H.R. 4314 on March 31 and April 1, 4, and 5. Congressional Record notice of introduction: https://www.congress.gov/73/crecb/1933/03/29/GPO-CRECB-1933-pt1-v77-17.pdf. Catalog record for the 248-page House hearing: https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1207663.
- **May 3, 1933 — H.R. 5480.** After those hearings, the House Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce prepared a new bill, H.R. 5480. The FTC's contemporaneous 1933 Annual Report records that chronology. As introduced, § 4(1) already began with the formulation "transactions by any person other than an issuer, underwriter, or dealer." The introduced bill also exempted transactions by an issuer "not with or through an underwriter." See the drafting history summarized against the House text in *SEC v. Ralston Purina Co.*, 346 U.S. 119, 122-23 (1953): https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/346/119/.
- **May 4, 1933 — H.R. Rep. No. 85.** The House Committee favorably reported H.R. 5480 and proposed adding "and not involving any public offering" after "underwriter." Primary source: H.R. Rep. No. 85, 73d Cong., 1st Sess. (1933): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-09774_00_00-042-0085-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-09774_00_00-042-0085-0000.pdf.
- **House Report's theory.** At p. 15, H.R. Rep. No. 85 says § 4(1) "broadly draws the line between distribution of securities and trading in securities" and explains that the Act is principally concerned with distribution rather than trading. It then says the paragraph therefore exempts transactions except those by an issuer, underwriter, or dealer. This is the clearest contemporaneous explanation of why those three categories appear in the statute.
- **May 5, 1933 — House floor.** The Clerk read the committee amendment adding "and not involving any public offering." Rayburn said: "This simply clarifies the language." The House adopted it and passed H.R. 5480. Primary Congressional Record, 77 Cong. Rec. 2910, 2954 (1933): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1933-pt3-v77/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1933-pt3-v77-9.pdf.
- **May 20-23, 1933 — conference.** The Senate had substituted its own version, after which the conference committee produced the final text. Primary source: H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 152, 73d Cong., 1st Sess. (1933): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-09774_00_00-075-0152-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-09774_00_00-075-0152-0000.pdf.
- **May 27, 1933 — enacted.** The Securities Act became law, ch. 38, 48 Stat. 74. Original § 4(1) included the same first clause: transactions by any person other than an issuer, underwriter, or dealer. A readable scan/transcription of the original Act is available through FRASER: https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/securities-act-1933-5878/fulltext.

The original definition of **dealer** is also worth retaining when reading the three categories. Original § 2(12) defined a dealer broadly to include a person engaged, directly or indirectly, "as agent, broker, or principal" in the business of offering, buying, selling, or otherwise dealing or trading in securities issued by another person. Thus the 1933 statute did not need to list "broker" as a fourth category in § 4(1); broker activity was expressly included within the original dealer definition.

## The FTC actually administered this system

The FTC, not the later-created SEC, was the original administrator of the Securities Act. Its own annual reports are unusually useful primary sources because they record both the legislative chronology and what the first federal securities regulator actually did.

The FTC's 1933 Annual Report says the House committee prepared H.R. 5480 after the H.R. 4314 hearings; H.R. 5480 was introduced May 3, reported May 4 with H.R. Rep. No. 85, passed the House May 5, went through conference, and was signed May 27. It also records that the FTC organized a securities division, issued an interim registration rule on June 29, and promulgated its first general rules and registration form on July 6. FTC Annual Report 1933: https://www.ftc.gov/reports/annual-report-1933.

The FTC's 1934 Annual Report gives the clearest measure of the resulting operation. Between July 7, 1933 and the September 1, 1934 transfer to the SEC, the FTC received and examined **1,095 registration statements**; **794 became effective**, **154 were withdrawn**, **98 remained under examination**, and **49** were under stop, refusal, or consent-refusal orders. It says the legal staff answered about **25,000** oral, written, and telegraphic interpretive inquiries during the 14 months ending September 1, 1934. The securities division had **382** alleged-violation complaints docketed for investigation, transmitted evidence in **seven** cases to the Attorney General for possible criminal proceedings, and the Commission had instituted **five** federal court cases. The report identifies Maison-Pichel, Inc. as the first Securities Act court proceeding and says a permanent injunction issued February 13, 1934.

Primary FTC report: https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports_annual/annual-report-1934/ar1934_0.pdf. See especially the report's Part II, "Administration of the Securities Act of 1933," pp. 37-42. The report also says that when the SEC took over on September 1, 1934 it acquired the FTC securities staff, procedure, administrative machinery, rules, and precedents; 115 securities personnel were transferred.

## Courts using H.R. Rep. No. 85

These are especially useful because they show courts treating the House explanation as evidence of the original statutory design rather than as later SEC policy.

### *SEC v. Sunbeam Gold Mines Co.*, 95 F.2d 699, 701-02 (9th Cir. 1938)

This is the earliest strong judicial use found so far. The Ninth Circuit quoted H.R. Rep. No. 85, p. 15, including the statement that § 4(1) "broadly draws the line between distribution of securities and trading in securities" and that it therefore exempts transactions except those by an issuer, underwriter, or dealer. The case then used that history in construing the public/private offering distinction.

CourtListener opinion: https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1563277/securities-exchange-commission-v-sunbeam-gold-mines-co/

### *SEC v. Ralston Purina Co.*, 200 F.2d 85 (8th Cir. 1952), rev'd, 346 U.S. 119 (1953)

The Eighth Circuit reproduced the same *Sunbeam* passage quoting H.R. Rep. No. 85. The Supreme Court then independently went back to H.R. 5480 and H.R. Rep. No. 85 when construing the private-offering portion of original § 4(1), noting that the introduced House bill exempted issuer transactions not with or through an underwriter and that the reported bill added "and not involving any public offering." This part of original § 4(1) later became the separate private-offering exemption now found in § 4(a)(2), so it should not be confused with the modern § 4(a)(1) first clause. But the case is important because the Supreme Court itself treats H.R. 5480 and H.R. Rep. No. 85 as the relevant original drafting record.

Eighth Circuit CourtListener opinion: https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/230486/securities-exchange-commission-v-ralston-purina-co/

Supreme Court opinion: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/346/119/

### *Securities Industry Ass'n v. Board of Governors*, 807 F.2d 1052 (D.C. Cir. 1986)

The D.C. Circuit traced the same H.R. 5480 drafting change and then connected it directly to the FTC. It noted that the introduced House bill used "transactions by an issuer not with or through an underwriter," that H.R. Rep. No. 85 added "and not involving any public offering," and that the FTC, as the agency originally charged with administering the securities laws, contemporaneously interpreted statutory "distribution" as necessarily involving a public offering. The court then pointed to Congress's 1934 deletion of the redundant "not with or through an underwriter" language as consistent with that understanding.

CourtListener opinion: https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/480729/securities-industry-association-v-the-board-of-governors-of-the-federal/

### *Pacific Dunlop Holdings Inc. v. Allen & Co.*, 993 F.2d 578 (7th Cir. 1993)

The Seventh Circuit examined the competing House and Senate 1933 drafts in detail and again quoted H.R. Rep. No. 85's distribution-versus-trading language. Although the dispute concerned the scope of § 12(2), the opinion is useful because it reconstructs H.R. 5480 as passed by the House, the Senate substitute, and the conference resolution using the original legislative materials.

CourtListener opinion: https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/606711/pacific-dunlop-holdings-incorporated-a-delaware-corporation-v-allen/

## Original drafting material / archive leads

The strongest primary drafting materials located online are the H.R. 4314 House hearing, H.R. Rep. No. 85, the May 5 Congressional Record, and H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 152. A useful archival lead for material behind the bills is the **Adolf A. Berle Papers** at the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library. Box 20 lists "Federal Securities Bill: Correspondence, April 3, 1933-July 31, 1933," a **"Memorandum re Federal Blue Sky Act, with outlines, drafts, and notes of A. A. Berle, Jr., April 3, 1933,"** and copies of **H.R. 4314, H.R. 5480, and S. 875**. Finding aid: https://www.fdrlibrary.marist.edu/archives/collections/franklin/index.php?id=49&p=collections%2Ffindingaid.

Also worth locating in the compiled SEC legislative history is the **Henderson & Dean memorandum to Rayburn dated April 4, 1933**, which later SEC materials identify as part of the legislative history surrounding the early "isolated transactions" language. The standalone original has not yet been located in a clean open-access scan.

## Potential use in the comment

The statutory-history point is stronger than saying that the SEC later developed a resale exemption. Congress itself began in 1933 by separating _distribution_ from _trading_ and placed ordinary transactions by persons outside the issuer/underwriter/dealer categories on the trading side of that line. Rule 144 came decades later as a safe harbor for determining when a person is not engaged in a distribution and therefore is not an underwriter; it did not create the underlying § 4(a)(1) exemption.

That history also makes the original FTC material unusually relevant. The agency administering the Act in 1933-34 was interpreting and enforcing a congressional statutory distinction that already existed before the SEC was created.
