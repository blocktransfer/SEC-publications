You’re probably thinking of **Securities Act Release No. 33-4552, “Nonpublic Offering Exemption” (Nov. 6, 1962)** — not a no-action letter. ([SEC][1])

It discusses the old practice of having purchasers sign an **“investment letter”** representing that they are acquiring the securities **for investment and not with a view to distribution**. The SEC explained that this representation, often coupled with a restrictive legend and stop-transfer instructions, could help substantiate reliance on the private-offering exemption. ([Library of Congress Tile][2])

But there’s an important distinction from the way you phrased it: **the investor cannot simply waive the benefits of registration and thereby make the offering exempt.** Release 33-4552 expressly says the investment representation is not conclusive and that these precautions “are not to be regarded as a basis for exemption from registration.” The exemption instead depends on the circumstances of the offering and, following *SEC v. Ralston Purina*, whether the offerees have the relationship/access to information that makes the protections of registration unnecessary. ([SEC][1])

There’s also a statutory reason a literal waiver would not work: **Securities Act §14, 15 USC 77n**, makes void any provision binding a purchaser to waive compliance with the Securities Act or SEC rules.

The closest match I can now pin down is an actual SEC staff **no-action letter, *Elwill Development Limited*, available Aug. 15, 1974**, reported as **BNA Securities Regulation & Law Report No. 205, at C-41 (Aug. 28, 1974)**. A contemporary analysis describes the transaction as unusual because the securities could be **publicly advertised**, but the ultimate sale was confined to one sophisticated purchaser who received the relevant information and gave an **investment letter**. SEC staff said it would not recommend enforcement under §5 provided, among other things, that the purchaser bought for its own account and could not publicly resell without registration or an exemption. ([Core Fileserver][1])

That is materially closer to what you remembered than Release 33-4552. The requester's theory was expressly that this could be treated as a **“public offering leading to a private sale”** because there was no practical need for registration or the public benefits of registration were too remote. ([Core Fileserver][1])

There is also an important nearby line of BNA material. Commissioner Hugh Owens's 1972 NASD speech was reported at **152 BNA Sec. Reg. & L. Rep. G-2 (May 17, 1972)**. He explained that merely giving someone disclosure and having them sign a letter saying they received/read it was *not* sufficient; the offeree still had to have the requisite access to material information. The Fifth Circuit later quoted that discussion in *Woolf v. S.D. Cohn & Co.* ([Law Resource][2])

So I think the thing you remember is probably **one of these BNA-indexed private-offering no-action letters around 1972–74**, rather than Rule 146 itself. *Elwill* is the strongest hit so far.
