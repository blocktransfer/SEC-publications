# Dentzer, the federal-depository proposal, and the House/Senate bank-regulator split

This note separates two related but distinct fights that William Dentzer compresses together in the oral history:

1. whether Congress should create a federally chartered national securities-depository corporation at all; and
2. if private depositories were used instead, whether the SEC would regulate them alone or share examination, supervision, and enforcement with the Federal Reserve and the other federal banking agencies.

Those are not the same proposal. The first is clearest in S. 2551 (1971). The second runs through the 1972-75 House/Senate bills and ultimately shaped the Securities Acts Amendments of 1975.

## Dentzer: the earlier "federal organization" alternative

The earlier Dentzer passage is explicit that a federal depository organization was being discussed:

> Now prior to this point, there had been some talk of a federal solution to this problem. In other words, creating some federal organization that would handle securities deposits.
>
> Yes. Congressman John Moss was in favor of that. His view as a Californian, as a liberal Democrat, was you can't trust New York at all. And, you know, there was a lot to that point of view. Since the private sector couldn't get its act together, some federal solution was desired, thought Moss.

Dentzer then says BASIC took the threat of a federal solution seriously and pushed a private-sector solution.

## The actual federally chartered national corporation: S. 2551

The clearest legislative proposal matching the "federal organization" concept was Senator William Roth's S. 2551, introduced September 20, 1971 as the National Securities Act.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/92/crecb/1971/09/20/GPO-CRECB-1971-pt25-1.pdf

Roth proposed a "COMSAT-type" National Securities Corporation (NSC). It is important not to overstate what "federal" meant here. The bill did not propose an ordinary government-owned agency. It proposed a congressionally created and federally chartered, privately owned for-profit corporation with substantial federal involvement.

The bill would have:

- created the National Securities Corporation by federal statute;
- directed it to establish and operate a national securities depository system;
- directed it ultimately to establish a national securities transfer system using certificateless ownership;
- provided a nine-member board divided between three presidentially appointed/Senate-confirmed directors, three directors elected by customer shareholders, and three elected by noncustomer shareholders;
- used private common stock as the ownership base;
- authorized Treasury to purchase up to $1 million of the corporation's obligations as startup financing; and
- subjected the corporation to SEC rules, reports, and examinations.

In other words, this was not simply "DTC, but federally regulated." It was a proposed national corporation created by Congress to operate the depository/transfer infrastructure itself.

### Relation to transfer agents

Roth expressly left open whether the NSC would absorb existing transfer-agent duties. His introductory statement says the clearing/settlement functions then performed by brokerage houses and limited depositories would move to the NSC, but whether transfer-agent functions would also be assumed was a question for the Securities Subcommittee.

That makes S. 2551 directly relevant to the transfer-agent history: Congress was considering a national corporation whose statutory mandate reached a national securities transfer system, while the sponsor was explicitly asking whether existing transfer-agent functions should be folded into it.

### Moss's connection to S. 2551: narrower than Dentzer's recollection may imply

There is direct evidence that Moss supported a national securities depository and was aware of S. 2551, but the evidence located here does not show that Moss sponsored Roth's NSC corporate design.

On May 9, 1972, Moss argued for a national depository with participation outside New York and said that, as a national depository was established, the National Commission on Uniform Securities Laws "as outlined in S. 2551" would be valuable.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/92/crecb/1972/05/09/GPO-CRECB-1972-pt13-4-2.pdf

That supports Dentzer's recollection that Moss favored a federal solution in the broad sense, but it should not be rewritten as "Moss introduced the National Securities Corporation." S. 2551 was Roth's bill.

## The competing private-system approach

The private-industry alternative was already developing in parallel: expand CCS/DTC and regional depositories, connect them, and regulate the resulting network rather than create one federally chartered national operator.

This distinction is visible even in the federal government's response to S. 2551. In May 1972 the Federal Reserve Board opposed creating the new federally chartered NSC and instead favored legislation broadening SEC authority over the evolving clearing/depository system.

So the historical choice was not "Federal Reserve national depository versus SEC national depository." The earlier choice was closer to:

- a new federally chartered national operator (S. 2551); versus
- an interconnected private depository system, subject to federal regulation.

The second model won. DTC and other private depositories developed, and the later legislative fight became a fight over who regulated them.

## Dentzer: the later House/Senate regulatory split

Dentzer separately describes the later fight this way:

> There were two competing bills, the House bill and the Senate bill. The House bill reflected Congressman John Moss's view of things. The Senate bill was the bill that BASIC, DTC and the SIA favored. I'm not sure that the differences were fundamental in my view, but they were considered to be fundamental from the point of view of banks. Banks wanted to be sure that the Federal Reserve was involved in the regulation of the depository, and they sought to carve out a role for the Federal Reserve in terms of the safety and soundness of any securities depository.

He immediately cautions that his recollection of the exact House provision was not clear.

## What "Federal Reserve involvement" actually meant

This was not merely a concern about duplicative SEC examinations. The bills allocated legal authority differently.

### 1972: S. 3876 versus the House approach

Federal Reserve Chairman Arthur Burns's October 12, 1972 letter comparing Senate S. 3876 with House H.R. 16946 spells out the institutional differences.

Under the Senate approach:

- Federal bank regulators retained the principal supervisory/enforcement role for banking institutions performing clearing/depository/transfer-agent functions.
- The Board of Governors had a defined role over bank holding companies and relevant nonbank subsidiaries.
- A bank acting as a transfer agent registered with its appropriate banking agency rather than with the SEC.
- Certain enforcement steps against bank clearing agencies were assigned to the appropriate banking regulator.

Burns objected that the House approach did not preserve the same Board role for bank holding companies and relevant subsidiaries, required bank transfer agents to register with the SEC, and allowed more direct SEC involvement in bank examination/enforcement.

Primary source:
- Arthur F. Burns to Harrison A. Williams Jr., Oct. 12, 1972:  
  https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1970/1972_1013_SparkmanBurnsLegislation.pdf

This is the concrete substance behind Dentzer's later shorthand about banks insisting that the Federal Reserve have a safety-and-soundness role.

### Moss's own House approach: centralized SEC authority

Moss's own 1972 H.R. 14826 went further toward consolidated SEC jurisdiction than the SEC's own H.R. 14567 proposal.

H.R. 14826 was introduced by Moss and Broyhill "to provide for the regulation of securities depositories, clearing corporations, and transfer agents."

Congressional Record / Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/92/crecb/1972/10/18/GPO-CRECB-1972-pt29-2.pdf

In explaining the competing proposals, Moss distinguished the SEC proposal, which divided regulation of bank transfer agents among the banking agencies, from his own proposal placing regulation of both bank and nonbank transfer agents with the SEC.

This is important because it identifies the underlying policy axis: Moss favored a more unified SEC-centered securities-processing regime; the banking industry wanted banking institutions to remain inside the bank-regulatory framework for safety-and-soundness supervision.

## 1973 makes the split even clearer: H.R. 5050 versus S. 2058

The same issue reappeared more starkly in 1973.

The SEC itself described the central question as whether examination and enforcement over clearing agencies, depositories, and transfer agents organized as banks should rest with the bank regulators or the SEC.

Its written comments stated that Title IV of H.R. 5050 would make the SEC the "sole regulator" of clearing agencies, depositories, and transfer agents.

Primary source:
- SEC comments on H.R. 5050:  
  https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1970/1973_0102_HR5050.pdf

The Senate's S. 2058 took the other institutional approach: the SEC retained broad national rulemaking authority, but federal banking agencies retained examination, supervision, and enforcement responsibility over banking institutions in their jurisdictions.

That is much closer to the arrangement Dentzer says BASIC, DTC, SIA, and the banks wanted.

## How this carried into the actual 1975 House/Senate bills

Dentzer's later "House bill" and "Senate bill" refer to the 1975 vehicles, not S. 2551.

### House: H.R. 4111

H.R. 4111 was the Securities Reform Act of 1975. Congress's own explanation said H.R. 4111 differed from the prior H.R. 5050 in only two important respects, neither involving the clearing/depository/transfer-agent regulatory architecture.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/04/17/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt9-1-1.pdf
- https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/04/24/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt9-6-2.pdf

That supplies the missing lineage from Moss's SEC-centered H.R. 5050 approach into the House's 1975 bill.

### Senate: S. 249

S. 249, the Securities Acts Amendments of 1975, carried forward the Senate clearing-agency/transfer-agent framework. Its description expressly contemplated shared responsibility: SEC authority would be exclusive in some areas and shared with bank regulatory agencies in others.

For transfer agents, a bank would register with its appropriate bank regulator while nonbank transfer agents would register with the SEC; the SEC would retain broad rulemaking power.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/01/17/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt1-4-2.pdf

That is the specific institutional difference Dentzer is describing when he says the banks cared about a Federal Reserve role.

## The 1975 compromise

The enacted Securities Acts Amendments of 1975 did not create the S. 2551 National Securities Corporation.

Instead, Congress regulated private clearing agencies/depositories and divided governmental responsibility through the "appropriate regulatory agency" structure.

For a clearing agency or transfer agent, Exchange Act Section 3(a)(34)(B) allocated the banking side by institution:

- OCC for national banks and specified subsidiaries;
- Federal Reserve Board for State member banks, bank holding companies, and specified subsidiaries;
- FDIC for insured nonmember banks and specified subsidiaries; and
- SEC for other clearing agencies and transfer agents.

Exchange Act Section 17A then required cooperation between the SEC and the banking agencies. Where a bank regulator was the appropriate regulatory agency, it received primary examination/enforcement responsibility, while the SEC retained the national securities-law rulemaking structure and its statutory enforcement role.

The statute also expressly required the national clearance-and-settlement framework to be consistent with "sound banking practices." That phrase is the closest statutory analogue to Dentzer's description of the safety-and-soundness concern.

Primary source:
- Public Law 94-29, 89 Stat. 97:  
  https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-89/pdf/STATUTE-89-Pg97.pdf

## Bottom line

The chronology matters:

1. S. 2551 (1971) proposed an actual federally chartered National Securities Corporation to operate national depository/transfer infrastructure. It was privately owned, but federally created, partly federally governed, initially Treasury-supported, and SEC-supervised.
2. Moss supported a national depository/federal solution in broad terms, but the source located here does not establish that Roth's NSC corporate design was Moss's own bill.
3. The private-depository model prevailed instead of the NSC.
4. The next fight was regulatory: Moss's House approach pushed toward unified SEC control, while Senate/industry/banking proposals preserved a separate Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC safety-and-soundness and enforcement role for banking institutions.
5. The 1975 Act adopted the private-system model plus divided SEC/bank-regulator oversight. It did not establish the federal National Securities Corporation.

Dentzer's oral history therefore compresses a multi-year sequence. His "federal organization" recollection points to the earlier national-operator debate; his later House/Senate quote points to the separate dispute over how the private depository system would be regulated.
