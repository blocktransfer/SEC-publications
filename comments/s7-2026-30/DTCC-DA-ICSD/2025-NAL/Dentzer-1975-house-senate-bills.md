# Dentzer on the 1975 House/Senate split and the Federal Reserve role

## Dentzer's recollection

From the [William Dentzer oral-history transcript](../../../../references/Dentzer-interview/transcript.md):

> There were two competing bills, the House bill and the Senate bill. The House bill reflected Congressman John Moss's view of things. The Senate bill was the bill that BASIC, DTC and the SIA favored. I'm not sure that the differences were fundamental in my view, but they were considered to be fundamental from the point of view of banks. Banks wanted to be sure that the Federal Reserve was involved in the regulation of the depository, and they sought to carve out a role for the Federal Reserve in terms of the safety and soundness of any securities depository.

Dentzer immediately qualified the next part of his recollection: he said his memory was not clear and that he did not think the House bill gave the Federal Reserve the same role. That caution matters. The record strongly substantiates the existence and importance of the bank-regulator dispute, but the precise 1975 House/Senate comparison should be tied to the actual bills rather than relying only on Dentzer's memory.

## The 1975 bills Dentzer was describing

The House vehicle was **H.R. 4111, the Securities Reform Act of 1975**. During the April 24, 1975 House debate, Chairman Harley Staggers introduced John Moss as the former chairman of the Subcommittee on Commerce and Finance "under [whose] leadership" the subcommittee had fashioned the legislation. Moss then described H.R. 4111 as the product of more than three years of committee study.

- Congress.gov, House debate, Apr. 24, 1975:  
  https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/04/24/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt9-6-2.pdf

The Senate vehicle was **S. 249, the Securities Acts Amendments of 1975**. When Senator Harrison Williams introduced S. 249 on January 17, 1975, he explained that it consolidated four Senate bills from the prior Congress, expressly including **S. 2058**, the Senate's clearing-agency and transfer-agent legislation.

- Congress.gov, introduction of S. 249, Jan. 17, 1975:  
  https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/01/17/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt1-4-2.pdf
- Congress.gov, Senate Daily Digest identifying S. 2058 as legislation to regulate securities clearance agencies and transfer agents, July 30, 1973:  
  https://www.congress.gov/crecb/1973/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt35-Pages550-554.pdf

On April 24, 1975, after passing H.R. 4111, the House took up S. 249 and voted to strike the Senate text and insert the House-passed H.R. 4111 text. The competing versions therefore went to conference in a particularly literal way.

## Direct evidence that the bank/Federal Reserve issue was real

The clearest contemporaneous evidence predates the final 1975 vehicles and shows exactly the institutional disagreement Dentzer remembered.

On October 12, 1972, Federal Reserve Chairman Arthur Burns wrote Senator Harrison Williams comparing the Senate's **S. 3876** with the House's **H.R. 16946**, both dealing with depositories, clearing agencies, and transfer agents. The Board of Governors expressly preferred the Senate bill with respect to banks, bank holding companies, and their nonbank subsidiaries.

The letter identified concrete differences:

- the House bill did not provide the Board of Governors the same supervisory role over bank holding companies and their nonbank subsidiaries that S. 3876 did;
- the House bill allowed SEC examination of banks in some circumstances, which the Board viewed as potentially duplicative;
- for a bank acting as transfer agent, the Senate bill provided for registration with the appropriate banking agency, while the House bill required registration with the SEC; and
- for certain clearing-agency enforcement steps, the Senate bill assigned action to the appropriate regulatory agency while the House bill assigned it to the SEC.

This is direct primary-source support for Dentzer's point that the division between SEC authority and traditional bank-regulator authority was treated as important by banking interests. It also shows that transfer agents were part of the same jurisdictional dispute.

- Federal Reserve Board letter from Arthur F. Burns to Harrison A. Williams Jr., Oct. 12, 1972, preserved by the SEC Historical Society:  
  https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1970/1972_1013_SparkmanBurnsLegislation.pdf

The Federal Reserve Board's own minutes for October 12, 1972 likewise record that staff presented the two bills and that the Board considered S. 3876 preferable to H.R. 16946.

- Federal Reserve Board minutes, Oct. 12, 1972:  
  https://files.crisesnotes.com/NT50972.pdf

## How that issue carried into the 1975 settlement

The final Securities Acts Amendments of 1975 did not make the SEC the sole regulator of every depository or transfer agent. Congress enacted a divided "appropriate regulatory agency" structure.

For a clearing agency or transfer agent, new Exchange Act Section 3(a)(34)(B) assigned the appropriate regulatory agency according to the institution:

- Comptroller of the Currency for national banks and certain subsidiaries;
- Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System for State member banks, bank holding companies, and specified subsidiaries;
- FDIC for insured nonmember banks and certain subsidiaries; and
- SEC for other clearing agencies and transfer agents.

New Section 17A(d)(3) then required the SEC and the appropriate bank regulator to consult and cooperate where the SEC was not the appropriate regulatory agency. The bank regulator was given primary responsibility for examination and enforcement, while the statute preserved the SEC's securities-law rulemaking and enforcement authority. The statutory objective expressly joined **sound banking practices** with the national system for prompt and accurate clearance and settlement.

That is a close statutory match to what Dentzer described as banks seeking a Federal Reserve role for the "safety and soundness" side of securities depositories. It was not a transfer of the national clearing system to the Fed; it was a dual/functional allocation of authority between the SEC and the federal banking agencies.

## Assessment of the Dentzer quote

**Strongly substantiated:** the House bill was H.R. 4111 and was shaped under Moss's Commerce and Finance subcommittee leadership; the Senate bill was S. 249; S. 249 incorporated the prior Senate clearing-agency/transfer-agent bill S. 2058; and the allocation of authority between the SEC and federal bank regulators was a real and repeatedly contested issue.

**Especially strong corroboration:** the 1972 Burns letter independently documents the Federal Reserve's preference for the Senate approach and identifies the same issues Dentzer later remembered: bank supervision, examinations, transfer-agent registration, and allocation of enforcement authority.

**Keep Dentzer's caveat:** his statement that he did not think the 1975 House bill provided the desired Federal Reserve role was expressly offered as an uncertain recollection. The predecessor-bill record shows that exact House/Senate split very clearly in 1972, while the enacted 1975 compromise ultimately preserved a substantial Federal Reserve/bank-regulator role.
