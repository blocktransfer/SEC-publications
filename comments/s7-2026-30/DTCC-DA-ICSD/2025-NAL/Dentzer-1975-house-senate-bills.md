# Dentzer, the federal depository proposal, and the House/Senate bank-regulator split

This note separates two related but distinct fights that William Dentzer compresses together in the oral history:

1. whether Congress should create a federally chartered national securities corporation to operate the depository/transfer infrastructure; and
2. after the private-depository model emerged, how authority over bank depositories and bank transfer agents should be divided between the SEC and the Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC.

Those were not the same proposal. The first is most concrete in S. 2551 (1971). The second runs through the 1972-75 House/Senate bills and is resolved explicitly in the 1975 conference report.

## Dentzer: the earlier federal-organization alternative

Dentzer first describes a proposed federal operator:

> Now prior to this point, there had been some talk of a federal solution to this problem. In other words, creating some federal organization that would handle securities deposits.
>
> Yes. Congressman John Moss was in favor of that. His view as a Californian, as a liberal Democrat, was you can't trust New York at all. And, you know, there was a lot to that point of view. Since the private sector couldn't get its act together, some federal solution was desired, thought Moss.

Dentzer then says BASIC took the prospect seriously and pushed a private-sector solution.

- [William Dentzer oral-history transcript](../../../../references/Dentzer-interview/transcript.md)

## The actual federally chartered national corporation: S. 2551

The clearest legislative embodiment of the "federal organization" concept was Senator William Roth's S. 2551, introduced September 20, 1971 as the National Securities Act.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/92/crecb/1971/09/20/GPO-CRECB-1971-pt25-1.pdf

Roth described the proposal as a "COMSAT-type, privately owned and funded, National Securities Corp." The distinction matters: it was not a conventional government-owned corporation. It was a congressionally created and federally chartered private corporation with public-law governance and federal oversight.

Title I would have created a National Securities Corporation (NSC) whose statutory purposes included:

- establishing and operating a national securities depository system;
- later establishing a national securities transfer system based on certificateless ownership;
- using modern technology to provide national securities-transfer services;
- implementing recommendations of the proposed National Commission on Uniform Securities Laws; and
- promoting competition through a national transfer system.

The proposed corporate structure was mixed public/private:

- nine directors;
- three appointed by the President with Senate confirmation;
- three elected by customer shareholders;
- three elected by noncustomer shareholders;
- private common-stock ownership;
- SEC rulemaking, reporting, and examination authority; and
- authority for Treasury to purchase up to $1 million of NSC obligations for startup financing.

So if "national government company" means a national operator created by Congress, S. 2551 is the proposal. If it means a wholly government-owned company, that description would be too strong. Roth deliberately modeled it on COMSAT: a federally chartered but privately owned corporation.

### S. 2551 and transfer agents

The NSC proposal reached directly into the transfer-agent question. Roth said the corporation would assume clearing/settlement functions then performed by brokerages and limited depositories, but he expressly left for hearings whether the NSC should also take over existing transfer-agent duties.

That is why S. 2551 belongs in the transfer-agent history: Congress was considering a federally chartered national operator whose mandate already included a national securities transfer system, with the sponsor explicitly asking whether existing transfer-agent functions should be absorbed as well.

### The Federal Reserve did not support creating the NSC

This is a useful separation from the later "Fed oversight" fight.

On May 5, 1972, the Board of Governors described S. 2551 as creating a "new federally-chartered National Securities Corporation" and said the objectives would be better served by broadening SEC authority under S. 3297/S. 3412 than by creating the new corporation.

Federal Reserve Board minutes and attached statement:
- https://files.crisesnotes.com/NT50909.pdf

So "federally chartered national corporation" and "Federal Reserve safety-and-soundness oversight" were not the same policy. The Fed itself opposed the former while later seeking a bank-regulatory role over private bank depositories.

### Moss and the NSC proposal

There is direct evidence that Moss favored a national depository/federal solution and engaged with S. 2551, but the sources located here do not show that Moss sponsored Roth's particular NSC corporate structure.

On May 9, 1972, Moss discussed establishment of a national securities depository and said the National Commission on Uniform Securities Laws "as outlined in S. 2551" would be valuable.

Congress.gov:
- https://www.congress.gov/92/crecb/1972/05/09/GPO-CRECB-1972-pt13-4-2.pdf

Accordingly, Dentzer's recollection supports tying Moss to the broader federal-solution pressure, but S. 2551 itself should remain identified as Roth's bill.

## The private-depository model displaced the NSC proposal

The competing model was not another government corporation. It was the development of CCS/DTC and regional depositories, linked into a national network and subjected to federal regulation.

That is the model the later legislation assumes. The 1975 fight was therefore no longer primarily "Should Congress create the NSC?" It was "Who regulates the private clearing/depository system, and in what capacity?"

This distinction is essential to reading Dentzer's later quotation.

## Dentzer: the later House/Senate regulatory split

Dentzer separately says:

> There were two competing bills, the House bill and the Senate bill. The House bill reflected Congressman John Moss's view of things. The Senate bill was the bill that BASIC, DTC and the SIA favored. I'm not sure that the differences were fundamental in my view, but they were considered to be fundamental from the point of view of banks. Banks wanted to be sure that the Federal Reserve was involved in the regulation of the depository, and they sought to carve out a role for the Federal Reserve in terms of the safety and soundness of any securities depository.

He then adds that his recollection of the precise House provision was not clear.

## The exact 1975 difference: the conference report

The best source for what Dentzer was remembering is not an inference from the 1972 or 1973 bills. It is the 1975 conference report itself.

H.R. Rep. No. 94-229 states:

> "A central difference ... concerned the authority and the responsibility of the bank regulatory agencies."

The report also makes an important correction to any simplistic "House = SEC, Senate = banks" description: **both** the Senate bill and House amendment gave the SEC general rulemaking and policy oversight over clearing agencies and transfer agents. They differed in how much authority was allocated to the federal bank agencies.

Conference report, H.R. Rep. No. 94-229, at 103-104:
- https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/Library/document/0055/12006521.pdf

The report identifies two concrete differences.

### 1. Examination of bank clearing agencies/depositories

The Senate bill gave the banking regulator the stronger institutional position.

Under the Senate bill:

- when the SEC was not the "appropriate regulatory agency" because the entity was a bank, SEC examinations were restricted;
- for a registered bank clearing agency, an SEC examination outside Exchange Act Section 21 had to be connected to a registration application, proposed rule change, or SEC rulemaking;
- the examination also had to be necessary or appropriate to the SEC's statutory responsibilities; and
- the examination was limited to matters relevant to that purpose.

The conference compromise removed that enumerated list of permissible SEC-examination circumstances, but expressly retained the Senate principle that **primary responsibility for examination rested with the appropriate bank regulatory agency**. It also required the SEC to give prior notice to and consult with the bank regulator so examinations could be coordinated.

This is much more than an objection to duplicative examination. It determines which regulator is primary for prudential examination of a bank depository.

For a State member bank, that "appropriate regulatory agency" could be the Federal Reserve Board. Thus this is one concrete legal mechanism for the Fed role Dentzer described.

### 2. Books, records, and reports of bank transfer agents

The second difference was specifically about transfer agents organized as banks or bank subsidiaries.

Under the Senate bill:

- the appropriate bank regulatory agency set the recordkeeping and reporting standards for bank transfer agents; and
- the SEC set minimum reporting/recordkeeping standards for all other transfer agents.

Under the House amendment:

- SEC requirements would also apply to bank transfer agents; and
- the bank regulators could impose additional requirements, but only consistently with SEC rules.

The conference compromise split the authority: **both the SEC and the bank regulatory agencies received rulemaking authority over the books, records, and reports of bank transfer agents.**

That is an unusually direct statutory example of the institutional issue Dentzer was describing. The dispute was not merely about whether the Fed could inspect DTC. It extended to who could prescribe operating-record requirements for bank transfer agents.

### Additional Senate bank provisions

The conference report also notes that the Senate bill extended the lost/stolen/counterfeit-security and fingerprinting provisions to insured banks and members of the Federal Reserve System, while the House amendment lacked a comparable provision. The House receded to the Senate.

That was not the principal supervisory dispute, but it is another example of the Senate bill expressly integrating banking institutions into the federal securities-handling framework.

## S. 249 shows what "safety and soundness" meant in the Senate model

The 1975 Senate bill, S. 249, makes Dentzer's phrase concrete.

For registered clearing agencies and transfer agents, it contemplated two different kinds of rulemaking:

- SEC rules directed to the public interest, investor protection, and the securities laws; and
- rules of the "appropriate regulatory agency" directed to the **safeguarding of securities and funds**.

It also provided that an appropriate bank regulator could make a safeguarding determination affecting whether a bank clearing agency could be registered.

Congress.gov, S. 249, Jan. 17, 1975:
- https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/01/17/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt1-4-2.pdf

That is the clearest statutory translation of Dentzer's phrase "safety and soundness": the SEC remained the national securities-policy regulator, while the traditional bank regulator retained a distinct role concerning the bank entity, safeguarding assets, examination, and enforcement.

## The House bill was not simply "SEC only" by 1975

The earlier history is still useful, but it must not be substituted for the actual 1975 comparison.

### 1972 and 1973 predecessors

In 1972, Federal Reserve Chairman Arthur Burns objected to the House approach because the Senate's S. 3876 preserved a stronger bank-regulatory role, including:

- Board authority over relevant bank holding companies/subsidiaries;
- bank transfer-agent registration with the appropriate banking agency;
- a larger bank-regulator role in enforcement; and
- tighter limits on SEC examination of banks.

Primary source:
- Arthur F. Burns to Harrison A. Williams Jr., Oct. 12, 1972:  
  https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1970/1972_1013_SparkmanBurnsLegislation.pdf

Moss's own 1972 H.R. 14826 likewise represented the more SEC-centered side of the debate.

In 1973, H.R. 5050 was more explicit still. The SEC described Title IV as making the SEC the "sole regulator" of clearing agencies, depositories, and transfer agents.

SEC comments on H.R. 5050:
- https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1970/1973_0102_HR5050.pdf

### But H.R. 4111 had moved by 1975

The actual House-passed H.R. 4111 cannot simply be described as the H.R. 5050 "SEC-only" model. By 1975 the House text itself recognized the OCC, Federal Reserve Board, and FDIC as "appropriate regulatory agencies" for specified banking institutions.

The remaining House/Senate disagreement was therefore the narrower but still consequential allocation described by the conference report: **primary bank examination authority and rulemaking over bank-transfer-agent records/reports.**

Congress.gov, House consideration of H.R. 4111 / S. 249, Apr. 24, 1975:
- https://www.congress.gov/94/crecb/1975/04/24/GPO-CRECB-1975-pt9-6-2.pdf

## The enacted compromise

The Securities Acts Amendments of 1975 did **not** create the National Securities Corporation proposed in S. 2551.

Instead, Congress regulated the private clearing/depository network and enacted the "appropriate regulatory agency" structure.

For a clearing agency or transfer agent, Exchange Act Section 3(a)(34)(B) allocated the bank-regulatory side by institution:

- OCC for national banks and specified subsidiaries;
- Federal Reserve Board for State member banks, bank holding companies, and specified subsidiaries;
- FDIC for insured nonmember banks and specified subsidiaries; and
- SEC for other clearing agencies and transfer agents.

Exchange Act Section 17A then required consultation and cooperation between the SEC and bank regulators. Where a bank regulator was the appropriate regulatory agency, that agency had primary examination/enforcement responsibility, while the SEC retained the national securities-law rulemaking structure and statutory enforcement authority.

The statute also directed the national clearance-and-settlement system to be consistent with "sound banking practices."

Primary source:
- Public Law 94-29, 89 Stat. 97:  
  https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-89/pdf/STATUTE-89-Pg97.pdf

## Bottom line

The two Dentzer passages describe different stages:

1. **1971 federal-operator proposal:** S. 2551 would have created an actual federally chartered National Securities Corporation to run national depository/transfer infrastructure. It was a COMSAT-style private corporation created by federal statute, not a wholly government-owned company.
2. **The Fed opposed that NSC model:** in 1972 the Board preferred expanding SEC authority over an evolving national network rather than creating the federal corporation.
3. **Private depositories prevailed:** CCS/DTC and other private depositories became the operating model.
4. **The later fight was regulator allocation:** by 1975 both House and Senate bills gave the SEC general securities-policy oversight, but the Senate protected a stronger prudential role for the Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC.
5. **The exact 1975 differences were concrete:** the Senate made the bank regulator primary for examinations of bank clearing agencies and gave bank regulators the recordkeeping/reporting authority for bank transfer agents; the House gave the SEC a larger role in both areas.
6. **The conference compromise preserved both:** bank regulators retained primary prudential examination/enforcement responsibilities, while the SEC retained national securities-system rulemaking and policy authority.

That is the connection Dentzer's shorthand leaves implicit: the proposed federal **operator** disappeared, but a federal **regulatory structure** survived in which the SEC directed the national securities-handling system and the Federal Reserve and other bank regulators retained institution-specific safety-and-soundness authority.
