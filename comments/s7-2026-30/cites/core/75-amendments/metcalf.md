* Senator Metcalf was making a much stronger “look through Cede” argument than the later SEC study usually gets quoted for. In 1974 he explained that DTC sent issuers monthly reports identifying participants and their holdings, and argued regulators should require issuers to use that data instead of reporting a giant Cede & Co. block. He expressly said DTC was cooperating because Cede itself did not have voting or discretionary rights. ([Abacus][1])
* Metcalf also documented how multiple nominee accounts could defeat percentage thresholds. He said Chase Manhattan actually held more than 6% of several airlines, but none of its separate nominee accounts crossed the 5% reporting threshold. His conclusion was that 5%, 10%, even 1% thresholds could miss control and that regulators should instead identify the largest voting blocks and aggregate related nominee holdings. ([Abacus][2])

There’s also a striking Metcalf line of thought that I think deserves its own note: the legally registered holder was almost beside the point; what mattered for corporate control was who could buy, sell, and especially vote the block. That is a substantially different conceptual framework from treating “record ownership” as the important endpoint.
