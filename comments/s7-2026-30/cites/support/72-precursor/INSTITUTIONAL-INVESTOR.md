> The New York Stock Exchange’s Central Certificate Service, a classic example of the dispatch with which Wall Street embraces new ideas and copes with change, was literally over a half century in the making. As early as 1913, Big Board representatives visited the Giro-Cassen Verein, a depository operated by the Vienna Stock Exchange, which enabled members to deliver and receive cash and securities by bookkeeping entry. “In this way,” the exchange noted in a report, “the responsibility of the member is relieved, as he has not sent any cash or securities through the streets from one house to another.” Several studies and pilot projects were conducted over the next several decades, but the depository concept, already operational in several foreign exchanges, was resisted by Big Board members who, among other things, feared erosion of their independence and relations with customers. Not until 1962, after the Street had experienced its first major paper crisis, did the board of governors authorize implementation of a “Central Stock Bookkeeping Service.”
