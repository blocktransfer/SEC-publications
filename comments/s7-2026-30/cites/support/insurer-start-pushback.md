

In 1971, Nevada law, for example, required domestic insurers generally to keep their assets within Nevada and required certain securities to be deposited through the insurance commissioner or an approved Nevada custodian. That closely corroborates Dentzer’s description, although his wording compresses several different requirements—asset location, regulatory deposits, custody, and admitted-asset treatment—into one general statement. [1971 Nevada insurance legislation](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/Statutes/56th/Stats197109.html)




***


The strongest direct corroboration is New York’s 1975 approval of insurers’ participation in DTC. The Insurance Department expressly required:

- records identifying the insurer’s holdings;
- securities to remain subject to the insurer’s instructions;
- immediate withdrawal upon demand;
- custodian responsibility for replacing lost securities or their value; and
- special affidavits when a custodian redeposited securities at DTC.

Without those protections, the Department would not recognize the securities as admitted assets. [New York Insurance Circular Letter No. 1 (1975)](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/industry_guidance/circular_letters/cl1975_01)



***







That makes Dentzer’s statement credible, but it also reveals what is missing from the reassuring version of the story: regulators did not simply decide that DTC possession was equivalent to the insurer possessing its own securities. They constructed a substitute made from account records, contractual duties, indemnification, withdrawal rights, and regulatory control.

The modern Article 8 problem sharpens that distinction:

- Under [UCC § 8-501(c)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/UCC/8-501), a security entitlement can exist even when the intermediary does not itself hold the corresponding financial asset.
- Under [UCC § 8-503](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/UCC/8-503), the entitlement holder generally has a pro rata interest in the intermediary’s pool—not an interest in an identified certificate or particular share. Enforcement ordinarily runs against the holder’s immediate intermediary.
- Article 8 nevertheless supplies meaningful protection: [§ 8-504](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/UCC/8-504) requires the intermediary to maintain enough corresponding assets, while [§ 8-507](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/UCC/8-507) requires compliance with authorized entitlement orders.
- Those protections are not absolute. [§ 8-511](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/UCC/8-511) contains priority rules that can favor a secured creditor with control when the available assets are insufficient.
