The concrete tradeoff looks like this:

The strongest case **for removing the postcard** is that the postcard is itself only a *notice* that the real proxy materials are online. The 2007 notice-and-access regime was adopted specifically to reduce the cost of mailing full proxy packages, but it still preserved a separate physical mailing. The July 2026 proposal would let that notification move electronic too, while retaining Internet access to the actual proxy materials and a right to request paper. ([SEC][1])

That potentially removes a meaningful layer of **printing, postage, handling, and intermediary processing expense** without removing the underlying disclosure. The SEC identifies those savings as one of the principal economic benefits of Reg E-Delivery. ([SEC][2]) And the SEC’s May 2026 investor survey found that about **79.5% of investors preferred some form of electronic delivery for disclosures without personal information**, although 87.7% also said investors should be able to choose between paper and electronic delivery. ([SEC][3])

The strongest argument **for retaining some physical notice** is different: the postcard has a *salience* function. It puts a tangible item in somebody’s mailbox saying, effectively, **“you have a shareholder meeting and something to vote on.”** An electronic statement can be missed, filtered as spam, sent to a stale address, or buried among routine brokerage messages. The proposal therefore shifts some risk from physical-delivery cost to electronic-notice effectiveness. The SEC itself asks about accessibility and whether the proposed safeguards are sufficient. ([SEC][4])

There is also a separate change I think is analytically more important than the postcard itself: **the proposal removes Rule 14a-16’s 40-calendar-day advance-notice requirement.** The replacement statement of availability would operate under the new timing framework instead. ([SEC][5]) That changes not merely *how* shareholders are notified but potentially **how much time they have to notice the solicitation, request paper, read it, and vote**.

So, if you're assessing this for a comment letter, I would separate the questions:

**(1) Is a mandatory physical postcard necessary?**
The economic rationale is weaker in 2026 because the postcard contains little substantive information, most investors prefer some form of e-delivery, and shareholders retain paper-access rights.

**(2) Should eliminating the postcard also mean eliminating the 40-day timing safeguard?**
That's a much less obvious proposition. You could eliminate the physical mailing while still requiring the electronic availability notice sufficiently early to make the paper-request right meaningful.

That distinction may actually give you a fairly strong comment-letter position: **the physical medium and the advance-notice safeguard don't have to rise and fall together.**
